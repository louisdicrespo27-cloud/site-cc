Refusing an inheritance in Portugal: what to know before you sign

Updated November 16, 2026

Possible debts, a distant property no one in the family wants, paperwork that seems endless: sometimes an heir living in the United States would rather refuse an inheritance in Portugal. Portuguese law allows it, but a refusal (repúdio) is final, and its effects often surprise people.

This guide sets out the Portuguese rules to know before you sign, where Portuguese law governs the estate. Which law governs is explained in our guide to inheritance in Portugal.

Final, and all or nothing

  • Retroactive: a person who refuses is treated as never having been called to the inheritance, except for the purposes of representation, explained below (Article 2062 of the Civil Code).
  • No conditions, no partial refusal: an inheritance cannot be refused on a condition or for a period of time, nor only in part — for example, keeping the bank accounts and refusing the house (Article 2064) — except in the particular case of an heir called both by a will and by law.
  • Irrevocable: a refusal cannot be withdrawn (Article 2066). It can be annulled for fraud or duress, but not for a simple mistake (Article 2065).

Do you need to refuse because of debts?

Not necessarily. In Portugal, an heir is never liable for the debts of the person who died beyond the value of what they inherit (Article 2071 of the Civil Code). The difference lies in who has to prove it:

  • if the inheritance is accepted with the benefit of inventory — by requesting an inventory or joining one already under way (Article 2053) — only the inventoried assets answer for the debts, unless the creditors prove that other assets exist;
  • if it is accepted outright, it is for the heir to prove that the estate is not enough to pay the debts.

Refusing is therefore not the only way to be protected. Where the debts are uncertain, acceptance with the benefit of inventory is worth considering.

Too late if you have already accepted

An inheritance that has been accepted can no longer be refused: acceptance is irrevocable (Article 2061). And acceptance can be tacit, inferred from conduct (Article 2056(1)). It is express when, in a written document, the heir declares that they accept, or takes the title of heir with the intention of acquiring the inheritance (Article 2056(2)).

Simple acts of administration, on the other hand, do not amount to tacit acceptance (Article 2056(3)). Before doing anything with the assets of the person who died, it is worth knowing which side of that line it falls on.

Refusing "in favor" of someone: the common trap

A person who declares that they refuse an inheritance in favor of only some of the heirs who would be called in their place is treated as having accepted the inheritance and then transferred their share (Article 2057(2) of the Civil Code). That is no longer a refusal, but an acceptance followed by a transfer, with its own consequences.

By contrast, giving up one's share free of charge in favor of everyone who would receive it if one refused does not amount to acceptance (Article 2057(1)).

Where your share goes: possibly to your children

Many heirs refuse thinking that their share will go to their brothers and sisters. That is not always what happens. Portuguese law provides for representation: the descendants of an heir who did not want to accept are called to take that heir's place (Article 2039). In intestate succession, it always applies in favor of the descendants of a child of the person who died, and of the descendants of a brother or sister of the person who died (Article 2042).

In practice, if you refuse your father's estate, your own children may be called in your place. If they are minors, you cannot refuse on their behalf without authorization (Article 1889(1)(j) of the Civil Code), which as a rule falls to the Public Prosecutor's Office (Decree-Law 272/2001), save in particular cases.

The form of a refusal

A refusal follows the form required to transfer an inheritance (Article 2063). If the estate includes assets that can only be transferred by public deed or by authenticated document — as is the case with real estate — the refusal must take one of those forms; otherwise, a written document is enough (Article 2126).

From the United States, this is usually done through a power of attorney that expressly includes the power to refuse the inheritance and takes the form Portuguese law requires. A power of attorney signed in the United States generally needs an apostille and a translation; signing it at a Portuguese consulate is often simpler.

Deadlines

The right to accept an inheritance lapses after ten years, counted from the moment the heir knows they have been called to it (Article 2059(1)). But waiting is not always possible: if a known heir neither accepts nor refuses, any interested party or the Public Prosecutor's Office can ask the court to have them notified to declare, within a set period, whether they accept or refuse. Without an answer in that period, the inheritance is treated as accepted (Article 2049).

The inheritance reform passed in 2026, which is not yet in force, would cut the period to accept from ten years to two. See the 2026 reform.

If you have creditors of your own

The creditors of a person who refuses can accept the inheritance in that person's name, within six months of learning of the refusal (Article 2067). Once those creditors are paid, what remains does not go to the person who refused, but to the next heirs.
