Making a will in Portugal: what Americans should know

Updated November 16, 2026

Many Americans who buy a home in Portugal assume their US will already covers it. Sometimes it does, at least in part. But Portuguese law has its own rules on what a will can do, on its form and on who must inherit — and a will that works perfectly in Florida or California can leave the Portuguese side of the estate uncertain.

This guide sets out the Portuguese rules that matter when you plan a will, and how they interact with a will made in the United States.

Do you need a Portuguese will?

Not always. Under Article 27 of EU Regulation 650/2012, a written will is formally valid if it complies with, among other options, the law of the place where it was made, the law of the testator's nationality, domicile or habitual residence, or — for real estate — the law of the place where the property is. A US will can therefore be valid in Portugal.

Formal validity, however, does not settle who inherits. And a will that has to be proved, translated and explained to a Portuguese notary or registry is slower to use than a Portuguese will. A Portuguese public will is written by the notary in the notary's own book (Article 2205 of the Civil Code), so your heirs do not depend on finding the original.

The forms of will in Portugal

Portuguese law has two ordinary forms of will (Article 2204 of the Civil Code), and both involve a notary:

  • The public will (testamento público), written by the notary in the notary's book (Article 2205);
  • The sealed will (testamento cerrado), written and signed by the testator — or by someone at the testator's request — and then approved by a notary (Article 2206). The date of approval counts as the date of the will (Article 2207).

Two rules surprise Americans in particular:

  • No joint wills. Two or more people cannot make a will in the same document, whether in each other's favor or in favor of someone else (Article 2181). Each spouse makes their own.
  • No will by proxy. A will is a personal act and cannot be made through a representative (Article 2182(1)). Unlike most other steps in Portugal, this one requires you in person.

The reserved share: what a will cannot take away

If Portuguese law governs your estate, part of it is reserved for your closest family — your spouse, your descendants and, if there are no descendants, your ascendants (Articles 2156 and 2157). A will can only dispose freely of what is left. With a spouse and children, for example, the reserved share is two thirds of the estate (Article 2159(1)); with a spouse alone, one half (Article 2158). A forced heir can only be disinherited by will, with the cause expressly stated, and only on the grounds the law lists — essentially, a conviction for certain serious offenses against the testator or close family, or an unjustified refusal of maintenance (Article 2166). Lifetime gifts that exceed the freely disposable part can be reduced at the request of the forced heirs (Articles 2168 and 2169).

Choosing US law in your will

Under the EU Regulation, the law of the country where you have your habitual residence at death generally governs your whole estate (Article 21). If you live in Portugal, that will usually be Portuguese law, with its reserved share. The Regulation applies even when the law it points to is that of a country outside the EU (Article 20).

You can change that: a will may state that the law of the country of your nationality governs your estate (Article 22). Because the United States has several legal systems, the will has to make clear which state's law is meant; Article 36 of the Regulation addresses this. It is a point to settle with care when the will is drafted, not after.

Spouses and unmarried partners

Spouses who marry under the separation of property regime can, in a prenuptial agreement, mutually renounce their status as forced heir of each other (Article 1700(1)(c) and (3) of the Civil Code).

Unmarried partners are neither heirs by law nor forced heirs (Articles 2133 and 2157). A will is the way to provide for them, within the part of the estate that can be left freely. A partner in a de facto union recognized under Law 7/2001 is exempt from stamp duty on what they receive; beneficiaries outside the close family generally pay 10%.

Wills in two countries

Under Portuguese law, a later will that does not expressly revoke an earlier one revokes it only where the two are incompatible (Article 2313(1)), and an express revocation can only be made in another will or by public deed (Article 2312). When you have a will in each country, each should say clearly what it covers, so that one does not unintentionally revoke or contradict the other.

The inheritance reform passed in 2026, not yet in force, would give testators more room — for example, to designate which assets make up a forced heir's share and to appoint an executor with powers of partition. See the 2026 reform.
